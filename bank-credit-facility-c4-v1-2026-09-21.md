## 0 · Defined terms

As in the C1, C2 and C3 records: *flow*, *standing limit*, *draw*, *receipts share*, *account line*, *exception cell*, *standard tariff*, *migration*, *price condition*, *carrying check*, *shadow limit*, *back-test*, *release*, *stage*, *overlay*. New at C4:

- **Want block** — the customer's doubt about whether the value is real. Not a doubt about the need (the need for working money on the day a payment falls due is felt, not argued) but about whether this offer will deliver it.
- **Credibility gap** — the structural cause of an efficacy doubt when the claim comes from a party with a reason to overstate and a record of withdrawing. Resolved by giving the customer direct experience of the claim, not by more claims.
- **Category entry point** — the moment at which the customer is most receptive to the value: here, the day a scheduled payment exceeds the available balance. Called the *shortfall* below.
- **Limit history** — the standing limit shown alongside the twelve months of the customer's own shortfalls it would have covered, each with the date, the amount, what the balance was and what the limit was that day. The customer verifies the rule against events they lived through.
- **Verifiable message** — a claim the receiver can check at no cost against a record they already hold. The opposite of *cheap talk*, a claim the receiver can only take on trust.
- **Conventional additions used** — the running sum, per £1,000 drawn a year, of every cost line R4 onwards adds to the C1–C3 floor, read against the ⚠ £8 the counterfactual allows.

## SR1 — The customer's implicit efficacy theory of change

**What the customer believes decides whether they have money on the day a payment falls due.** Three beliefs, each observable in the published surveys:

1. **Working money is a decision about me, made by the bank.** Credit is granted after an application, by a person or a process reading the accounts, the history and the security. The main determinant of success is passing that assessment. Evidence: the SME Finance Monitor reports that of businesses that wanted finance and did not apply, the largest reasons are the expectation of rejection and the process itself (would-be seekers ⚠ 5% of SMEs; T2, BVA BDRC, 2024–2025).
2. **What the bank gives, the bank takes back.** A business overdraft is repayable on demand; every UK bank's terms say so and the Standards of Lending Practice for business customers state it as the norm (T1, Lending Standards Board). The customer's operating belief is that a limit is present until the day it is needed most, and may be cut at the annual review.
3. **Asking signals trouble, so do not ask.** The safer path is own money and not applying: 35% of SMEs used personal funds in the year, 22% because they felt they had to (T2, SME Finance Monitor); around half of SMEs are permanent non-borrowers by the Monitor's definition (T2).

**The implicit theory of change, in the customer's terms.** "I have money on the day if I keep my own buffer and do not depend on the bank. A bank limit is a decision I have not yet passed, and a decision they can reverse."

**The C3 form against this theory.** The form offers a limit that is a property of the account, needs no application and is not reviewed annually. Each of the three beliefs says this offer is not what it seems: belief 1 says a number with no decision is not money; belief 2 says it will be pulled; belief 3 says drawing on it exposes me. The form removes the operations the beliefs were formed on, but it does nothing, as it stands at C3, to change the beliefs. That is the want block.

**Where the belief is strongest.** The C2 selection named four candidate customers. The standard-tariff overdraft user meets the limit at migration (C2): the limit replaces an overdraft they already hold at a price below it, so belief 1 is answered by the retirement of the overdraft and belief 2 by the saving statement. The want block is strongest for the customers who do not hold an overdraft today: the owner-funded (⚠ 1.25m businesses, 22% "had to") and the deterred would-be seeker (⚠ 285,000). For them the limit is a number from a bank they have chosen not to ask. R4 designs for them.

## SR2 — Key efficacy doubt

**Primary doubt type: efficacy doubt.** The need is felt: a scheduled payment larger than the balance is a problem the customer already solves, today, with own money, a late payment or an unpaid-item fee. The doubt is whether *this* offer solves it: "the number on the screen is not money; it will need an application when I try to use it, or it will be gone."

**Structural cause: credibility gap.** The claim ("you have £6,400 available, no application, no review") comes from a party the customer has reason to distrust on exactly this claim. Three grounds. (a) The bank benefits from the customer believing it (the tariff on a draw). (b) The record: overdrafts withdrawn on demand, "pre-approved" offers that turned into applications, limits cut at review (belief 2, SR1). (c) No instrument exists today by which the customer can check the claim before drawing. The only test is to draw, which is the act the doubt prevents. A claim that can only be tested by the act it is meant to induce is not testable; it is taken on trust or not at all.

**Secondary causes, and why they are not primary.**
- *Inertia (status quo bias — Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty).* Present for the tariff overdraft user, who has a facility that works. Answered at C2 by migration: the overdraft is retired into the limit at a price below it, so the status quo moves without the customer acting. Not primary for the owner-funded and deterred customers, who hold nothing to be inert about.
- *Social risk.* Low: a draw on a standing limit is seen by no one outside the account. The old social risk — being seen to apply and be refused — is removed by the form (no application). Not primary.

**Specific manifestation in this segment.** The customer sees the limit, does not draw, and funds the shortfall as before. The limit is read as a marketing number. The test the customer would apply, if one existed, is: "would this have covered the VAT payment in March, when I put £2,300 of my own money in?" No instrument answers it at C3.

**Resolution class.** For a credibility gap the resolution is to reduce the cost of forming a verified belief: sampling, trials or outcome demonstrations that give the customer direct experience of the claim, in place of testimonials or advertising, which are more claims from the same party. The strategy at SR4 is chosen from this class.

## SR3 — Attraction theory of change

```
ATTRACTION THEORY OF CHANGE — R4
  Class of problem: persuasion with an unverifiable message — a claim from an interested party that the receiver can only check by the act the claim is meant to induce (cheap talk)
  Theory: Milgrom 1981, "Good News and Bad News: Representation Theorems and Applications", Bell Journal of Economics — when a message is verifiable at no cost to the receiver, the receiver believes it at face value and the sender's incentive to overstate has no purchase; the supporting mechanism is Hoch and Deighton 1989, "Managing What Consumers Learn from Experience", Journal of Marketing — evidence the customer generates from their own experience is held with more confidence than evidence supplied to them
  Context: the receiver holds a record of the events the claim is about (the customer's own account history — every shortfall, every payment, every receipt, on the same screen as the limit); the sender already computes the claim for every day of that record (the shadow limit and the back-test, C3); the receiver meets the claim at a moment of felt need (the shortfall)
  Mechanism: the claim is presented as a history the receiver can check against events they lived through — "this is the limit you would have had on 14 March, when the VAT payment was due and your balance was £2,300 short" — so the belief is formed by verification, not by trust, and the cost of forming it is a minute of reading
  Current state: the customer sees a number, has no way to test it except by drawing, and funds the shortfall as before
  Desired state: the customer holds a verified belief that the limit would have covered their own last twelve months of shortfalls, and draws on the next one
```

**Theory stress test.** Milgrom's result needs the message to be verifiable by the receiver at low cost and the record to be one the sender cannot alter. Both hold: the account history is the customer's own, already displayed, already relied on for tax and for paying suppliers; the bank cannot rewrite a past balance. The result does not need the customer to understand the rule, only to check its outputs against dates they remember. Crawford and Sobel 1982 (Econometrica) is the contrast case: with an unverifiable message and diverging interests, the receiver discounts the message toward nothing — which is the observed state at C3. The theory is from information economics, a low-risk field; neither paper is on the fragile-findings list.

**What the theory does not say.** It does not say the customer will draw once convinced — that is a separate block (R5, by number). It does not say a customer with no shortfalls in twelve months will be convinced; for them the history is empty and the theory offers nothing. It does not say the customer will trust the bank in general; only this claim, on this record. And it says nothing about price (C6, by number).

Conviction is by inference: the tariff user infers the limit is real because their overdraft was retired into it (C2); everyone else is left with the number. There is no conviction mechanism for the customer who has never held a facility. The C1 CTM row "aware → convinced the limit is real" has been flagged `[REQUIRES C4+]` since C1.

**Attraction strategy (one structural move, three sentences).** The limit is disclosed as its own verifiable history. At release the account shows the limit beside the twelve months of the customer's own shortfalls it would have covered, each with the date, the amount, the balance that day and the limit that day. The history is computed from the shadow limit and back-test the form already runs. The statement is placed in front of the customer on the day the flow shows a scheduled payment larger than the available balance, the moment the need is felt. Nothing is sent, bought or campaigned; the conviction mechanism is a read of a ledger the bank already holds, on a screen the customer already reads.

**Mechanism.** The structural cause is that the claim can only be tested by drawing. The history gives the customer a test that costs a minute and needs no draw: "would this have covered March?" The answer is on the screen, against the customer's own record, which the bank cannot rewrite. Verification replaces trust (Milgrom 1981); the evidence is the customer's own (Hoch and Deighton 1989).

**Entry point.** The shortfall: a direct debit, standing order or scheduled payment due within the Bacs cycle (three working days, T1) that exceeds the available balance. The bank sees it before the customer does. This is the day the customer is deciding between own money, a late payment and an unpaid-item fee; receptivity to working money is at its peak and the history answers the question the customer is asking that day. For the migrated tariff user the entry point is the migration statement (C2), where the history appears beside the saving.

**R1 consistency.** The move is a presentation of the C1 rule's output, on the C1 account, at a moment the C1 flow reveals. It adds no operation to the delivering zone; it removes one from the conventional form (marketing). Consistent.

**Hypothesis.** If the limit is shown as its own twelve-month shortfall history at the shortfall, then the share of released never-borrowed accounts (owner-funded and deterred) that draw within ⚠ 90 days of their first shortfall after release is at least ⚠ 3 times the share that draw when shown the number alone. **Falsified if** the ratio is below ⚠ 1.5 at ⚠ 10,000 accounts in each arm (a pre-registered bands comparison inside Stage 2, C3 — the two replicas allow one arm each), or if fewer than ⚠ 30% of released accounts have any shortfall in the prior twelve months (an empty history convinces no one). **Named assumptions:** (1) the back-test history is per account and per day, so a twelve-month shortfall list can be produced for each released account (T3 — the back-test is designed that way at C3; its readable form is C3's unmeasured input); (2) the bank can identify a scheduled payment before it settles (T1 — Bacs advance submission; standing orders are the bank's own); (3) the display of a limit and its history to a sole trader is a financial promotion under CONC 3 and passes "clear, fair, not misleading" without a representative example, because no rate or cost is shown at C4 (T2 — CONC 3.5.5R requires the representative example only where a promotion indicates a rate or an amount of any charge; design position, runner's call; what a ruling would settle: whether the amount of the limit alone is "an amount of credit" that triggers CONC 3.5.5R(1)(b) — if it does, the representative example is added to the display at C6 by number, where the price is set); (4) a limit with an empty shortfall history is shown without the history and without a placement, and is counted as unconvinced (T3 — design position).

**Three tests.**
- *Primary cause.* The cause is the absence of an instrument to verify the claim before the act. The move supplies the instrument. It does not address a result of the cause (awareness, tone, reach). **Passes.**
- *Entry point.* The shortfall is when the customer is deciding how to fund a payment. Timed to it, not to a calendar. **Passes.**
- *BFF consistency and incumbent test.* A bank on the roadmap form (a pre-approved limit from a one-off assessment, insider 18 September 2026) cannot show a limit history: it has one limit, set once, with no daily rule to run backward over the account's past. A lender without the account cannot see the shortfall. The move needs C1 (the daily rule), C3 (the shadow ledger and back-test) and the account itself. **Requires this venture's architecture: yes.**

**Credibility mechanism (VA-81).** Receiver: the business owner (the owner-funded or deterred customer). Proposition: "the limit shown would have covered each of your last twelve months' shortfalls." Instrument the receiver uses today: the account's transaction history and balance, on the same screen — read for tax, for supplier payments and for reconciliation. Public-record supplier of the instrument: the bank's account line, under the Payment Services Regulations 2017 (regs. 53–56 — information on transactions the payer is entitled to), which every UK payment account provider supplies (T1). A second receiver exists for the same proposition: the conduct reviewer who signs the display as a financial promotion; instrument: the promotion sign-off record every credit firm keeps under CONC 3.3; supplier: the bank's compliance function.

## SR5 — Business form factor (C1–C4)

**Central structure.** A bank whose credit facility is a standing property of every account with enough flow, whose rule is proven on exposure the bank already holds before it is trusted, and whose limit is believed because the customer can check it against their own past.

*Cumulative BFF (C1–C4).* A bank whose credit facility is a standing property of every account with enough flow — a limit computed daily from the receipts and payments the bank already observes, drawn without an application and repaid by a fixed share of each receipt as it arrives — into which the customer's existing overdraft is retired at a one-time migration at a price held below the tariff; whose rule is proven before it is trusted (run first as a shadow limit on the whole base, back-tested on the bank's own history, released first where it is at or below the exposure the bank already carries, and released to the rest by stages against a pre-registered kill band); and whose limit is believed because it is shown as its own history — at release the account shows the limit beside the twelve months of the customer's own shortfalls it would have covered, placed in front of the customer on the day a scheduled payment exceeds the balance, so the customer verifies the rule on events they lived through before drawing on it. The bank keeps no per-application decision, no security process, no annual review, no collections operation and no marketing line — only a rule under review, a release order, a history the customer can check, and exception cells for accounts whose flow stops, whose holder declines, or whose holder asks why the limit is what it is.

**Read cold.** One sentence names what is made (a daily limit), how it is proven (on existing exposure), how it is believed (its own history), how it is sold (in the account, at the shortfall), how it is paid (the receipts share; price by number to C6) and what is not done (decision, security, review, collections, marketing).

**Fault-line check.** C1 says "every account with enough flow"; C3 says "released by stage"; C4 says "shown with its history at release". The unreleased account sees nothing — no shadow limit, no history — until its replica opens (*runner's call*: a limit the bank cannot yet honour would create its own credibility gap, the defect the move exists to remove). At release the history appears at once, because the back-test has already computed it. No fault line.

## Synthesis check (VA-1)

**Real-business analogue.** The venture now looks like Square Loans in the way it earns belief as well as in the way it computes the offer: the offer is shown inside the record it was computed from, to a customer already reading that record, who can check it against their own sales before accepting. **Limit of the analogue.** Square's seller has no history of credit withdrawn by Square; the bank's customer has.
